You're absolutely right. In the most popular version of the Prisoner's Dilemma, the choices are indeed **{confess, not confess}**. The terms "defect" and "cooperate" are the generalized game theory labels for these actions.

### 🎭 The Standard Prisoner's Dilemma Scenario

The classic context, as formalized by Albert W. Tucker, involves two criminals arrested for a crime. They are placed in separate interrogation rooms with no means of communication. The police offer each prisoner the same Faustian bargain, creating the following payoff matrix based on their combined choices:

| Your Choice | Other's Choice | Your Sentence | Other's Sentence |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Confess (Defect)** | **Confess (Defect)** | 2 years | 2 years |
| **Confess (Defect)** | **Not Confess (Cooperate)** | **0 years** | 5 years |
| **Not Confess (Cooperate)** | **Confess (Defect)** | 5 years | **0 years** |
| **Not Confess (Cooperate)** | **Not Confess (Cooperate)** | 1 year | 1 year |

In this structure:
- **"Not Confess" = Cooperate**: You cooperate with your partner by remaining silent.
- **"Confess" = Defect**: You defect from your partner by confessing to the police.

The dilemma is that while both players would be best off if they both cooperated (each getting 1 year), the rational, self-interested choice for each individual is always to confess (defect), leading to a worse collective outcome (each serving 2 years). This outcome is the game's **Nash Equilibrium**.

### 🔄 From One-Time to Repeated Games

A crucial extension of this model is the **Iterated Prisoner's Dilemma**, where the same two players face the same choices multiple times.

- **Single-Round Game**: Defecting (confessing) is the dominant strategy, as there is no future to worry about.
- **Repeated Rounds**: The logic changes because players can punish defection in future rounds. This "shadow of the future" makes cooperation a rational possibility.
  - The most famous successful strategy is **Tit-for-Tat**, which starts by cooperating and then simply mimics the opponent's previous move. This strategy is nice, retaliatory, forgiving, and non-envious, allowing it to foster and sustain cooperation.

The core of the Prisoner's Dilemma is the tension between individual and collective rationality. Would you like to explore more about how the Iterated Prisoner's Dilemma explains the evolution of cooperation?
